Production / Warehouse Productivity Dashboard

Motivation

At Golden State Assembly (GSA), we do something called "re-works" on some harnesses. Whenever an original equipment manufacturer (OEM) has some changes they'd like to make to their harnesses they'll send them to us along with the work instructions and any required materials. A schedule is agreed upon (for example: deliver 30 harnesses every Monday and we'll have them ready on Friday for pickup) and then work begins.

However for one client, they began picking up their finished harnesses less frequently. This became a problem for us because our client continued to deliver their harnesses weekly while not picking up their re-worked harnesses. These harnesses eventually accumulated, taking up valuable warehouse space.

As a result, management needed visibility on exactly how many harnesses were accumulating, and to compare these figures with other metrics such as production rates and inventory history. Now of course, this isn't new information for GSA. We already track this data and present it in tables through our internal portal, but it's quite cumbersome to access as each piece of information is on a separate webpage. Therefore, my goal was to create a cohesive dashboard that provides a clear picture of this specific issue while also maintaining flexibility for general use.

Technical

To build the dashboard, I used R Studio to draft my first Shiny App. The dashboard contains 3 separate plots: Daily Production, Quantity Produced vs Quantity Shipped, and Inventory History. Using reactive objects, I utilized dropdown menus and date ranges for user navigation throughout the dashboard. At the very top, the user selects the specific part number they would like to see which will automatically change all three plots to reflect that part number. Once that is done, the user is able to filter the data using date ranges.

Our organization has a central database and uses MSSQL to interact with it. Therefore on the backend I set up a connection between MSSQL and my R application through Open Database Connectivity (ODBC) within the R app. That way, I'm able to develop the front-end and call the SQL queries all within the same R script.

Solution

For privacy reasons, I've changed the actual part numbers and client names to generic names such as "PN 1", "PN 2", etc. The final product contains the three sections below one-another with each having their own filters where applicable. For visibility sake I've added some extra information such as a side-widget presenting the sum of the 1st plot's metrics and displaying the exact value of each bar within the second plot.

Internally, this gave management exactly what they needed to be able to monitor the situation. They are now able to visually check-in on multiple assemblies with just a few clicks rather than navigating multiple webpages and comparing tables of numbers. Designating where action needs to be taken has now become much easier.

Externally, we now had to get action from our client. Of course the easiest way is to incur fees for warehouse space, but this could damage the relationship with the client from a customer service perspective. The next best option is to effectively communicate the need for action from the client. In my experience, in order for something to get done outside of one's organization, one must convince the point of contact why it's important. With this dashboard, management was able to remotely meet with the client and visually present to them how quickly inventory was accumulating. After being able to see the scale of the problem, the client then took corrective action with the responsible parties within his organization. The dashboard played a critical role on all fronts.
